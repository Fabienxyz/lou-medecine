# Learning Profile Extraction

## Executive Summary

- Lou studies medicine by starting from annales/QCMs, not from the course alone.
- She wants reusable reasoning rules that work across variants.
- She becomes frustrated when summaries omit QCM-level details.
- She needs explanations that reconcile apparent contradictions.
- She prefers direct, exam-oriented answers over broad conceptual summaries.
- She challenges corrections when they conflict with logic or source details.
- She uses screenshots as anchors for targeted learning.
- She benefits from timing-based and sign-based decision rules.
- She wants “what to suspect first” rather than exhaustive medical lists.
- She dislikes generic fiches if they are not built from annale patterns.
- She validates learning by testing whether a rule explains multiple QCMs.
- She prefers concise answers when asking a narrow question.
- She needs clear distinction between “possible,” “probable,” and “priority.”
- She learns well through contrastive reasoning: why this and not that.

## 1. Learning Workflow

1. Lou provides course files and annales.
2. She asks for chapter-by-chapter high-yield summaries.
3. She tests those summaries against actual annales.
4. When a discrepancy appears, she brings a screenshot or exact QCM.
5. She challenges the explanation if it does not match the correction or her logic.
6. She asks for the underlying rule behind several similar QCMs.
7. She refines the rule herself and asks for validation.
8. She moves toward memorization only after the rule feels stable.

Evidence:

- “je veux que tu te bases sur ces sources pour répondre à mes questions”
- “je veux que tu me fasses en fait un méga résumé sur chaque chapitre”
- “quand je refais les annales, je vois qu'il y a énormément de choses qui manquent”
- “je ne comprends toujours pas pourquoi…”
- “Voilà ce que j'en ai déduit des annales”

## 2. Learning Preferences

### Annale-first learning

**Confidence:** HIGH  
**Evidence:** “reprends les QCM qui sont tombés les années précédentes”; “des trucs qui tombent de manière super fréquente”  
**Why it matters:** The product should start from past exam patterns before generating course summaries.

### Transferable rules

**Confidence:** HIGH  
**Evidence:** “résumé… de ce qu'il faut maîtriser et des trucs qui tombent”; “genre une fois… une fois… je ne comprends rien”  
**Why it matters:** Explanations should end with a reusable rule: “if X wording, then Y reasoning.”

### Contrastive reasoning

**Confidence:** HIGH  
**Evidence:** “Pourquoi est-ce que ça ne peut pas faire penser à une grossesse extra-utérine, mais plutôt à une fausse couche ?”  
**Why it matters:** The assistant should explain why wrong intuitions fail, not only give the correct answer.

### Source fidelity

**Confidence:** HIGH  
**Evidence:** “tu ne lis pas comme il faut mes documents”; “regarde la consigne”  
**Why it matters:** The companion must quote or reference the exact wording of QCMs when resolving disputes.

### Concision for narrow questions

**Confidence:** MEDIUM  
**Evidence:** “Va droit au but, réponds juste ça.”  
**Why it matters:** When Lou asks a targeted factual question, avoid long fiches.

### Structured summaries

**Confidence:** HIGH  
**Evidence:** “sous forme de tableau”; “bien précis et claires et synthétiques”  
**Why it matters:** Tables and rule cards should be default outputs.

## 3. Preferred Learning Assets

### Mega fiches built from annales

**Confidence:** HIGH  
**Evidence:** “méga résumé sur chaque chapitre”; “ce qu’il faut maîtriser et des trucs qui tombent”  
**Why she likes it:** It consolidates scattered QCM traps into one revision unit.  
**When useful:** End-of-chapter revision.

### Comparison tables

**Confidence:** HIGH  
**Evidence:** repeated requests to distinguish GEU vs fausse couche, fibrome vs kyste, immediate vs delayed complications.  
**Why she likes it:** It resolves confusion between similar options.  
**When useful:** Differential diagnosis and QCM traps.

### Decision rules

**Confidence:** HIGH  
**Evidence:** “à partir de quand on peut éliminer…”; “c’est quoi un peu la limite…”  
**Why she likes it:** It converts uncertain cases into exam actions.  
**When useful:** Clinical vignettes.

### Screenshots/QCM walkthroughs

**Confidence:** HIGH  
**Evidence:** multiple screenshots followed by “Je ne comprends pas ce QCM.”  
**Why she likes it:** The exact wording becomes the anchor.  
**When useful:** Error correction and high-yield trap extraction.

## 4. Recurrent Difficulties

### Apparent contradictions between QCMs

**Confidence:** HIGH  
**Evidence:** “une fois… c’est pas possible, une fois ils disent que c’est possible”  
**Product implication:** The tool should classify by question wording: possible / probable / priority / impossible.

### Missing QCM-level details in summaries

**Confidence:** HIGH  
**Evidence:** “je vois qu'il y a énormément de choses qui manquent”; “c’est une catastrophe”  
**Product implication:** Generate fiches only after extracting annale patterns.

### Trust breakdown when source wording is misread

**Confidence:** HIGH  
**Evidence:** “tu ne lis pas comme il faut mes documents, putain, regarde la consigne”  
**Product implication:** Use exact evidence snippets before explaining.

### Timing logic in clinical cases

**Confidence:** HIGH  
**Evidence:** repeated questions about “directement après,” “plus longtemps après,” “précoce/tardif.”  
**Product implication:** Use timelines and timing-based diagnostic tables.

### Probability vs certainty

**Confidence:** HIGH  
**Evidence:** “à partir de combien de temps… je peux directement éliminer une grossesse ?”  
**Product implication:** Explicitly separate “cannot exclude,” “less likely,” and “ruled out.”

## 5. Successful Pedagogical Patterns

### “Timing → suspicion” rules

Worked when Lou summarized ponction complications herself: immediate = hemorrhage/torsion; delayed = abscess/OHSS; fever = abscess.  
Technique: converting clinical timing into a decision table.

### “Question wording changes the answer”

Worked for métrorragies QCM confusion.  
Technique: separate “possible cause,” “most probable,” and “priority diagnosis.”

### “Exact correction challenge”

When Lou challenged the Rhésus QCM, the useful pattern was acknowledging the source contradiction and validating her logic.  
Technique: distinguish exam correction from biological reasoning.

## 6. Ineffective Pedagogical Patterns

### Generic chapter summaries

They failed when not derived from annales.  
Evidence: “quand je refais les annales, je vois qu'il y a énormément de choses qui manquent.”

### Overbroad reassurance

Saying “we covered it” was not enough. Lou later found missing QCM traps.

### Explanations that ignore exact wording

Misreading the Rhésus/Kell question damaged trust.  
Evidence: “Non mais là, tu ne lis pas comme il faut mes documents.”

### Long answers to narrow questions

Lou explicitly requested directness: “Va droit au but, réponds juste ça.”

## 10. Stable Insights

- Lou studies from exam questions first.
- She needs reusable rules more than isolated facts.
- She validates understanding against annales.
- She detects contradictions quickly and challenges them.
- She needs exact wording considered.
- She prefers structured, high-yield outputs.
- She benefits from comparison tables and decision trees.
- She wants the teacher/exam logic, not only medical truth.
- She distinguishes “clinically true” from “QCM expected answer.”
- She needs concise answers for narrow questions.
- She becomes frustrated when explanations are too generic.
- She learns by transforming examples into rules.

## 11. Contradictions

- Lou asks for mega summaries but later rejects summaries that are not granular enough for annales.
- She wants concise answers for narrow points, but comprehensive coverage for chapters.
- She wants course-based answers, but the annales sometimes contain questionable or inconsistent corrections.
- She accepts QCM logic but also challenges it when it conflicts with biological reasoning.
