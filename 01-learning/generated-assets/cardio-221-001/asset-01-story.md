---

# Imagine a healthy artery...

At the beginning, the inside of the artery is smooth.

Blood flows freely.

The endothelium (the thin inner lining of the artery) acts like a protective coating:

- it keeps the blood flowing normally;
- it prevents unnecessary clot formation;
- it controls what enters the arterial wall.

Everything is working normally.

---

# Step 1 — LDL particles begin to enter the arterial wall

LDL particles circulate continuously in the blood.

Normally, only a small amount enters the arterial wall.

However, over time—especially when LDL levels are high—more LDL accumulates inside the **intima**, the innermost layer of the artery.

At this stage, nothing dramatic has happened yet.

The LDL is simply trapped inside the wall.

---

# Step 2 — LDL becomes oxidized

Once trapped, LDL is exposed to free radicals.

These molecules modify the LDL.

The LDL is now **oxidized**.

This changes everything.

Why?

Because oxidized LDL is recognized as abnormal by the body.

It becomes a danger signal.

---

# Step 3 — The artery calls for help

The endothelial cells detect this danger.

They express adhesion molecules on their surface.

These molecules act like "Velcro."

They slow down circulating monocytes and allow them to stick to the vessel wall.

The monocytes then leave the bloodstream and enter the arterial wall.

---

# Step 4 — Monocytes become macrophages

Inside the arterial wall, the monocytes transform into macrophages.

Macrophages are immune cells.

Their normal role is to remove abnormal material.

They therefore begin to ingest the oxidized LDL.

---

# Step 5 — Macrophages become foam cells

The macrophages progressively fill with oxidized LDL.

Eventually they become overloaded with fat.

At this stage they are called **foam cells (cellules spumeuses).**

Large numbers of foam cells accumulate.

This creates the beginning of the lipid core.

---

# Step 6 — Inflammation becomes self-sustaining

The foam cells release inflammatory mediators.

These attract even more immune cells.

More inflammation leads to more endothelial dysfunction.

More endothelial dysfunction allows more LDL to enter.

A vicious circle has started.

The process now feeds itself.

---

# Step 7 — The artery tries to repair itself

The body attempts to stabilize the lesion.

Smooth muscle cells migrate from the media toward the intima.

They produce collagen and extracellular matrix.

This forms a fibrous layer over the lipid core.

This is the **fibrous cap (chape fibreuse).**

---

# Step 8 — A mature plaque has formed

The plaque now contains:

- a lipid core;
- foam cells;
- inflammatory cells;
- smooth muscle cells;
- a fibrous cap covering the lesion.

At this stage the plaque may remain stable for years...

...or later become unstable and rupture, which will be explained in the next paragraph.

---

# The story in one sentence

High LDL → LDL oxidation → immune response → foam cells → chronic inflammation → repair attempt → fibrous cap → atherosclerotic plaque.

---

# Key idea

Do **not** memorize the list.

Remember the story.

Each event naturally explains why the next one happens.
